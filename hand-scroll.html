Appendix — input

Scrolling with nothing but a hand

Wave a hand up and this whole page scrolls up; wave down and it goes down. Each flick gives the page a push proportional to how hard you waved, and it coasts to a stop — so your arm rests between gestures instead of holding a pose. Switch to Hold for the throttle behaviour, where height above or below centre sets a steady speed, or point the sensor at the demo panel instead of the page with the target switch. Every stage of the signal is on the right, from the raw landmark to the pixels per second going into scrollTop.

01

The camera is the clock

Frames arrive at about 30 fps; the display wants 60. Detection runs on requestVideoFrameCallback — the camera's own cadence — while scrolling is integrated once per animation frame. Polling the tracker from the render loop stalls both of them.

02

Twenty-one points, two of them useful

The model returns a full hand skeleton. This sensor reads the wrist and the middle knuckle and throws the rest away. Fingertips are the jitteriest landmarks on the hand — they swing with any wrist rotation, so steering from one feels broken even when tracking is perfect.

03

The camera is mirrored, the maths is not

A selfie camera flips X, so a hand moving right reports moving left. Y is untouched. Since this sensor only reads vertical motion the mirror never enters the control law — but the preview is flipped back in CSS so the picture matches the person.

04

Smoothing that knows when to stop

A One Euro filter smooths hard when the hand is nearly still, where jitter shows and lag does not, and backs off when it moves fast, where lag shows and jitter does not. A plain lerp strong enough to kill the shake also adds lag — which reads as slow and inaccurate at the same time.

05

The dead band is the whole trick

A hand held still is never actually still. Without a neutral zone around centre the panel creeps constantly and the sensor feels broken, however good the tracking is. The band is the difference between a control and a drift.

06

Why the response curve is not linear

Offset is raised to a power before it becomes velocity. That buys fine control near the band — small deliberate nudges stay slow — while the extremes still reach a few thousand pixels a second. A linear mapping gives you neither end well.

07

Integrate, don't jump

Velocity accumulates into position: scrollTop -= v * dt, with dt clamped so a stalled tab cannot fling the panel several seconds' worth of scroll on its first frame back. Position is never assigned from the hand directly — that is what makes it hold steady instead of twitching.

08

Losing the hand is a normal state

Hands leave frame constantly. A hard cutoff on the first missed detection makes the panel stutter, so misses are counted before the signal is dropped, and velocity eases to zero rather than snapping. Recovery should be invisible.

How it works — the signal chain

Seven stages between a webcam and a scrollbar

A camera frame is a long way from a scroll position. Each stage below throws away information the next one does not need, and the order matters — smoothing after the dead band, for instance, would smooth a signal that has already been snapped to zero.

Stage 01

Capture

A 640×480 stream at 30 fps. Detection is driven by requestVideoFrameCallback so it runs once per camera frame, never twice on the same one.

out → VideoFrame
Stage 02

Detect

MediaPipe's hand landmarker returns 21 3-D points. Everything except the wrist and the middle knuckle is discarded on the spot.

out → landmarks[21]
Stage 03

Reduce

The two survivors are averaged into a single vertical coordinate. A midpoint is far steadier than any single landmark, and rotating your wrist barely moves it.

out → rawY · 0–1
Stage 04

Filter

A One Euro filter adapts its cutoff to speed — hard smoothing when still, light when moving. Fixed smoothing has to pick one, and gets the other wrong.

out → y · de-jittered
Stage 05

Dead band

Offset from centre is measured, and anything inside ±0.08 becomes exactly zero. Without this the panel creeps forever, because a still hand is never still.

out → offset or 0
Stage 06

Curve

What survives is normalised and raised to a power, buying fine control near the band while leaving the extremes fast. This is the stage that decides how the sensor feels.

out → velocity · px/s
Stage 07

Integrate

Velocity is eased toward its target, multiplied by frame time and accumulated into scrollTop. Position is never written from hand position directly.

out → scrollTop · px

The control law

One line of arithmetic, four numbers you can move

This is the whole sensor. The curve below is drawn from the same function that drives the demo, so moving any slider up there redraws it — and while the camera is running, the marker shows exactly where your hand sits on it.

Response curve Dead band — velocity forced to zero Your hand, live

offset  = 0.5 − y
mag     = |offset| − dead    ← ≤ 0 means neutral, stop here
norm   = min(1, mag / (0.40 − dead))
v      = sign(offset) × normexp × maxV

scrollTop −= lerp(v, ease) × dt    ← dt clamped to 50ms

y is the hand's height in the frame, 0 at the top. Because a hand held high gives a small y, 0.5 − y comes out positive — so the sign of the offset is the scroll direction, with no branching. The four bold names are the four sliders.

Wave mode — the return stroke

Every wave up is also a wave down

Waving is the obvious gesture and the treacherous one. Flick your hand up and your arm has to come back — so the camera sees two motions of opposite sign, and a detector that simply reads direction cancels the first with the second. The page ends exactly where it started, and the sensor looks broken while working perfectly.

The fix is to make the detector edge-triggered rather than continuous. Three gates do it, and removing any one of them brings the problem back:

Gate 01

A trigger floor

Vertical hand speed has to cross a threshold before anything fires, so drifting, repositioning and general fidgeting never count as a gesture at all.

rejects → slow drift
Gate 02

A refractory window

Once a flick fires, the detector stops listening for 340 ms. A return stroke launched immediately after the flick lands inside that window and is never seen.

rejects → fast return
Gate 03

A re-arm floor

After the window closes the hand must actually slow down before the detector re-arms. A leisurely return that outlasts the window still cannot fire, because it never gets slow enough to re-arm mid-motion.

rejects → slow return

What survives is one push per deliberate flick, however untidily the arm comes back. The push is scaled by how hard you waved — up to 2.2× — and then simply coasts: velocity is multiplied by the glide factor every frame, raised to dt × 60 so the distance covered is the same on a 60Hz and a 144Hz display rather than stopping twice as fast on the faster one.

Wave or hold? Waving is better for travelling — a few flicks cross the document and your arm rests between them. Holding is better for arriving, because you can creep at ten pixels a second and stop exactly where you meant to. The switch is in the panel above.

Why a throttle

Three ways to point a hand at a scrollbar

The obvious mapping — hand height is scroll position — is the one that falls apart first. The problem is range: an arm covers about 40 cm, and the content does not care.

MappingHow it behavesWhy it was or wasn't used
Absolute Hand height maps straight onto scroll position, top of frame to top of document. 1:1 and obvious, but 40 cm of arm has to span the entire document — on a long page that is roughly 25 px of scroll per millimetre of hand. Tracking jitter is amplified by the same factor.
ThrottleIn use Hand offset sets scroll speed; velocity is integrated into position. Range stops mattering — any distance is reachable from one comfortable arm position, and the dead band gives a real resting state. The cost is that it needs a progress readout, since the hand no longer says where you are.
Pinch and drag Pinch to grab the page, move to drag it, release to let it coast. The most physical of the three and the closest to a real touch gesture, but pinch is the flakiest signal on the hand — a missed release leaves the page stuck to your palm.

Using it

Four things worth knowing before you wave

  1. Sit about an arm's length back with your palm facing the camera. The model wants to see a whole hand, not a fist or an edge-on profile.
  2. Wave up to go up, down to go down. One deliberate flick per push — you do not need to wave hard, and the return stroke is ignored, so bring your arm back however you like.
  3. Switch to Hold to arrive precisely. Waving is for travelling; holding just off centre lets you creep a few pixels a second and stop on the line you wanted.
  4. If waves are missed, raise sensitivity; if a single wave overshoots, lower the push or the glide. In Hold mode: widen the dead band if it drifts, lower the response curve if it feels sluggish to start.

The camera never starts on its own. Nothing is requested until you press the button, the video is processed entirely on this machine, no frame leaves the page, and Esc cuts the stream at any time — as does the Stop button in the corner readout, which stays on screen for exactly that reason while your hand is driving the page. The wheel, trackpad and arrow keys keep working throughout; nothing is captured or locked, so you can always take back control by hand. Hand tracking needs a network connection the first time — the model is fetched from Google's CDN, then cached by the browser.
